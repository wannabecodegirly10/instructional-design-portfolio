Capstone & Reflection

This course and the associated assignments and projects have expanded my knowledge of learning design, facets of digital environments, instructional design frameworks, emerging technologies including AI, data privacy, and the importance of effective, structured peer feedback. While I am not currently an educator or learning designer, taking this course following Instructional Design has increased my confidence in applying the skills I have learned in my current role and work. This course, along with the supplemental Coursera Google and Adobe courses and modules, enhanced my learning experience.

Beginning with our first assignment, I chose to analyze the Coursera course AI Foundations: Prompt Engineering with ChatGPT offered by Arizona State University to identify opportunities for equitable, accessible online learning. I was able to look more deeply at how digital environments work, identify the pros and cons of those environments, and offer feedback to make them better and align to the following standards: UDL: Engagement, Action & Expression, QM: Accessibility & Usability, the Accessibility Layer framework, and the WAVE Web Accessibility Evaluation Tool (CO1). The analysis and learning about what to look for gave me insight into scrutinizing the digital tools currently used by my institution for students, as well as the tools or vendors I will present to my leadership for review.

As I prepared to work on my assignment for Foundations of Instructional Design and UDL, I knew I wanted to find a course that would allow me to incorporate Backward Design, one of the frameworks that resonated with me from my Instructional Design course. Being able to incorporate it into the development of an inclusive online course using that framework was very rewarding (CO2). “Starting with the end in mind” makes sense to me. Having a clear understanding of the concrete concepts and knowledge students should take away from the course aligns with my general thinking and approach to learning, even if I was not able to articulate it a year ago. Once I determined what I wanted the students to take away from the course/module, I could think through the assessments and learning activities aligned with the learning outcomes.

The scaffolding of the assignments, which builds on previous work, is all in service to the final project. Design Quality, Ethics & Portfolio Review, the midpoint of the course, was the perfect transition from the second assignment. Through the Google AI Fundamentals Coursera course (CO4), I gained an insightful background to help me revise the Open Learning Initiative course Student Cognition Toolbox: Introduction to Study Strategies. AI and ethics initially seem like two components that do not coincide, but with rapidly evolving technology, it is important to examine how AI is used, how its use is disclosed, and the way it is used. Understanding and incorporating UDL standards is a non-negotiable in learning and instructional design. It is essential for every student to feel as though there are no roadblocks to the lesson objectives, assignments, or assessments (CO3).

I enjoyed experimenting with and analyzing the emerging technology tool Nearpod. Applying strategies for data privacy, cybersecurity, and responsible technology integration in my lesson helped me think more deeply about my lesson goals. Nearpod was also a great complement to my Backward Design framework. The non-intrusive, beta AI features helped me stay focused on my own ideas, adhere to Backward Design, and create an authentic lesson.

The last two assignments of the course, Assessment Strategies and Data-Informed Design and Interactive Media and Collaborative Selection, were closely related. Using tools and considering student privacy when creating a dashboard with meaningful but limited information ensures that student information is protected. In the final assignment, incorporating summative and formative assessments while using additional emerging technology tools, H5P and Padlet, brought together all aspects of my lesson and the redesign.

One aspect of the course that I found surprisingly valuable was the peer feedback. Having a structured approach and prompts to help facilitate thoughtful, respectful, and helpful feedback in the service of helping my peers improve my work and vice versa was truly engaging. I felt as though everyone had a genuine interest in seeing one another succeed and make our work better. While at times it felt overwhelming, access to Coursera courses and certificates provides additional opportunities to build skills and ongoing professional development (CO5). Overall, this course, peer feedback, and assignments that I poured time and intention into will help me become an intentional designer with a respect for Universal Design for Learning.

Midpoint Reflection: Design Quality, Ethics & Portfolio Review

I chose to use Backward Design for the revision of this lesson because the model allowed me to think about what I wanted students to walk away with at the end. The Backward Design model begins with the end in mind. Once I determined what I wanted the students to know I was able to develop actionable, non-vague learning objectives. From there, creating formative assessments and activities was easier to align and map to the objectives.

Understanding and incorporating UDL standards is a non-negotiable in learning and instructional design. It is essential for every student to feel as though there are no roadblocks to the lesson objectives, assignments, or assessments.

Peer-review and feedback have been helpful not only with the midterm project, but also through this course and my previous course. It is easy to have blind spots in our work, and I appreciate peer review and feedback because it provides various perspectives on my work. I also find it engaging when I’m able to share my peer-review with my classmates.

AI and ethics initially seem like two components that do not coincide, but with rapidly evolving technology it is important to examine how AI is used, that its use is disclosed, and the way it is used. Ethical considerations are abundant as it relates to AI because it can help us in our personal, professional, and educational lives. Any use of AI in work that is submitted as your own must be disclosed. AI can assist, but never do your work for you.

My next steps are to incorporate additional feedback from peers and instructor to continue to build on the work I have done.

Narrative of Emerging Technology

I chose to analyze Nearpod, an emerging technology tool that can be used to create lessons with additional tools to include interactive activities and assessments.

While I found the platform easy to use and navigate from a UX design, due to the volume of templates and lessons available to choose from, it can be a bit overwhelming for educators and designers who might not know where they want to start with a general design. The platform does meet UDL-Engagement, Representation, and Action & Expression; adherence to those same standards can also act as a barrier when there are too many options.

I enjoyed reviewing, demoing and using Nearpod. They also have a robust Privacy Hub, by their parent company, Renaissance. That hub provides detailed information about how they handle student and institutional data. It also contains their Customer and Data Protection Agreements with various countries.

  • TPACK Integration Model
  • UDL – Engagement: Offers videos, readings, and games in its templates and activity bank
  • UDL – Representation: Allows images, audio, video, and links to support access and viewing
  • UDL – Action & Expression: Activities and assessments accept responses in multiple formats, including audio, video, and images
  • QM – Accessibility & Usability: Templates support consistency in fonts, colors, accessibility settings, and contrast across slides
  • ISTE 2.2, 2.5, 2.7: Support my work as a Learning Designer by enabling accessible module design, flexible templates, and ongoing analysis of emerging tools and standards

View PDF Document

Narrative about Data Analytics Dashboard Selection

I selected Airtable for this data analytics dashboard because its granular sharing controls support responsible handling of student data, including the ability to restrict access to users with an lsu.edu email address via SSO. To further protect privacy, I focused on non-sensitive identifiers—Student ID, Completion Status, and Completion Date—and intentionally made Completion Status the primary metric using options such as Completed Assessment 1, Completed Assessment 2, Completed Both, and Extension Granted. The dashboard visualizes these data as percentage completion in a pie chart, complemented by a pivot table that offers a slightly more detailed view, allowing me to monitor assessment progress without exposing unnecessary student information. Alignment to Quality Matters Assessment Standards (QM 5.2) and CETL III Data Privacy & Security guidelines.

  • QM 5.2: Assessment Standards and CETL III Data Privacy & Security guidelines
  • UDL: 1.2: Alternatives for auditory information, 2.5: Multiple media icons, brief text, 3.3: Guide information processing and visualization

Narrative about Interactive Media & Collaborative Selection

I opted to use H5P for my artifact because I thought that interactive activity worked with the type of content I was using, not only for this assignment, but also for what I have built on throughout this term. Multiple-choice and discussion board prompts align with my original Backward Design model for this course. The tie-in with the Padlet discussion board helps foster collaboration because the choices within the H5P activity provide information to reinforce learning without blatantly calling out students as "failing" if they did not select a response directly aligned with one of the study strategies. The discussion board will allow students to directly apply their learning from the overall lesson and the summative checks within the H5P interactive activity, and allows them to share what they learned with their peers and also pick up some additional information from them as well, in an informal way. While the interactive activity is a good fit, that does not mean every student would feel the same way. They might struggle with remaining focused on viewing text-only throughout the activity. I was intentional about the information I used for the activity because I did not want it to be too dense, and therefore defeat the purpose of quick, "bite-sized" pieces of information that can be absorbed or re-visited as needed. This is an activity where I send a short feedback survey. One limitation of this medium is the need to link to another tool to complete the second phase of the activity. My goal was to ensure that part was seamless. Some students might experience issues with the link, or, depending on their device type, the information might render at a different size or cause formatting issues.

  • Clark & Mayer’s Principles: Coherence, Multimedia, Signaling, Redundancy
  • UDL-Multiple Means of Expression
  • QM5, QM6
  • ISTE 2.1, 2.4, 2.5
